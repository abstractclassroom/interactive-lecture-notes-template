## Countable and uncountable infinity

An infinite set is **countably infinite** when its elements can be placed in a one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$. Informally, its elements can be arranged in a sequence with a first item, second item, third item, and so on.

Representative countably infinite sets include

$$
\mathbb{N}, \qquad \mathbb{Z}, \qquad \mathbb{Q}.
$$

Although the integers and rational numbers may appear larger than the natural numbers, each has cardinality $\aleph_0$.

An infinite set is **uncountable** when no such correspondence with $\mathbb{N}$ exists. The real numbers are uncountable. Their cardinality is commonly denoted

$$
|\mathbb{R}| = \mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0}.
$$

In particular, $|\mathbb{N}| < |\mathbb{R}|$. Infinite sets therefore do not all have the same cardinality.
